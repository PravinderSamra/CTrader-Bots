# 01 — Liquidity Fundamentals

First-principles explanation of what "liquidity" means in this strategy, extracted from the three source transcripts in `../01-transcripts/`. Every claim cites its source as `(Video N, [MM:SS])`.

- **Video 1** = `video1_transcript.md` — Chart Fanatics interview with Marco Trades ("Marco"), the strategy's author.
- **Video 2** = `video2_transcript.md` — Chart Fanatics part two with Marco, the "Da Vinci model".
- **Video 3** = `video3_transcript.md` — Inter Equity Trading, same conceptual school and vocabulary.

---

## 1. What liquidity is

Marco's definition is deliberately minimal: **liquidity is just resting orders in the market** — above all, stop losses. "In my opinion, liquidity is just resting orders in the market... let's just say stop orders... the best example for myself, what I use the most, would just again be stop losses. If I understand certain traders are entering at a high or a low, then I'll understand that there's going to be resting liquidity there. It's essentially just understanding where those orders sit in the market" (Video 1, [3:59]–[4:36]).

Concretely: when traders enter long positions at/near a low, their stop-loss orders (sell stops) rest **below** that low. When traders enter shorts at/near a high, their stops (buy stops) rest **above** that high. Those clusters of resting orders are "liquidity pools", and they are what price gets driven into.

Liquidity is the organizing principle of the entire strategy: "Liquidity is everything for me. It gives me my direction. It gives me my bias, my entries, A to Z" (Video 1, [0:36], repeated [2:39]).

## 2. Buy-side vs sell-side liquidity

The transcripts rarely use the formal ICT terms "buy-side/sell-side liquidity", but the concept is constant:

- **Liquidity above a high** = buy stops of trapped sellers + breakout buy orders. Price is drawn *up* into it; it is what you **target when long** and what you **sell into / sell after it is swept** (Video 1, [3:22], [25:08]; Video 2, [16:42]–[17:05]).
- **Liquidity below a low** = sell stops of trapped buyers. Price is drawn *down* into it; it is what you **target when short** and what you **buy below after it is swept** (Video 1, [5:10]–[6:27]; Video 2, [13:46]–[14:35]).

The operating slogan is: **"Buy below lows, sell above highs"** (Video 1, [10:12]–[10:49]). You never buy *above* an identified low-side liquidity pool and never sell *below* an identified high-side pool — you wait for the pool to be taken first.

## 3. Why liquidity pools form: inducement

Pools form because **retail concepts genuinely (temporarily) work, and that is their function**: "these retail concepts are all used to build liquidity in the market... they temporarily work. They work for good reason, purposely to induce liquidity into the market. If these concepts never worked — smart money, support, resistance — then why would people trade them?" (Video 1, [8:59]–[9:33]).

The canonical inducement sequence (bullish version, Video 1, [4:36]–[5:10] and [8:19]–[9:33]):

1. Price takes out a prior high. Structure traders read this as a **BOS** (break of structure) / momentum signal — buyers are now "induced".
2. Price returns to a retail point of interest — order block, fair value gap, Fibonacci level, support, "breaker structure", whatever framework the retail trader uses (Video 1, [5:10]–[5:52], [7:08]–[7:46]).
3. Buyers enter there; the market moves away upward, sometimes even paying them briefly ("it might even work out in their favor", Video 1, [5:10]).
4. **Now there is liquidity below that low** — the stops of every buyer who entered. That pool is the fuel/target for a later move down.

The bearish version is the exact mirror (Video 1, [25:08]; Video 2, [24:52]–[28:38]).

Video 3 frames the same idea at higher-timeframe scale: a multi-week sell-off exists "to clear all of this liquidity" — a chain of old lows that had been tapped and built up "for weeks, months" (Video 3, [1:52]–[2:27]). "Think about the mentality of the retail trader seeing this kind of move... it's going to induce them to continue selling" (Video 3, [1:16]–[1:52]).

## 4. Why price is drawn to liquidity

The strategy's causal story: engineered order clusters give the market a *reason* to move to a level. "We have built reasoning for price to push up there. We have built liquidity... we have a reason for price to push above" (Video 2, [66:59]–[67:20]). Once buyers are trapped below and there is "a ton of liquidity to the downside, it is a great confluence... because now price has a reason to go lower" (Video 2, [68:51]).

Untaken liquidity does not expire — it becomes a **future target**: "almost always I can tell you that if we have left liquidity like this, it's going to be either a future target you can sell into or another buy opportunity somewhere down the line. Maybe it's the next day or even the next week" (Video 1, [25:49]–[26:28]). Marco also notes liquidity is often built the day *before* scheduled news: "if there is news on a Wednesday, this could occur on Tuesday... it's just built liquidity for news the following day" (Video 1, [26:28]).

Conversely, where there is **no** liquidity, there is nothing to trade toward. After a large one-directional move with no highs/lows left: "all I can mark on is we've taken out this high, period. I don't see any other liquidity. So why would I be trading?" — he may stay off that chart for days while liquidity rebuilds (Video 1, [14:32]–[15:06]). The host labels a one-directional stretch with no swing points a "liquidity void" (Video 1, [15:44]–[16:17]; host's term, Marco does not push back). Motto: **"If you don't see liquidity, you are the liquidity"** (Video 2, [0:22], [54:11]).

## 5. THE CORE RULE — real liquidity vs highs/lows with no liquidity

This is the single most load-bearing discrimination in the whole system. **"Not every high and every low has liquidity"** (Video 1, [0:00], [3:22]). Two symmetric rules:

### 5a. A level HAS liquidity when it is *respected* and price *moves away*

> "I understand that if I see the market respect a high from A to B and move away, that tells me there's liquidity above that high... Now we have approached the low down here. If we respect it and move away, perfect. Simple stuff. There's liquidity below that low." (Video 1, [13:13]–[13:56])

Pattern (bearish-side example): **high approaches a previous high, respects it (does not trade through), and the market moves away downward** → liquidity now rests above that high. Mirror for lows (Video 1, [3:22], [13:13]; Video 2, [25:49]–[26:39]; Video 3, [1:16], [5:24]).

Critically, this is *reactive, not predictive*: "I never like to predict where liquidity is going to be, highs or lows. I simply let the market communicate that to me" (Video 2, [5:24]–[5:47]; echoed Video 3, [1:16], [4:11], [14:06]: "We are not guessing. We are not predicting. We're waiting for the market to tell us this").

Why respect matters: the respect *is* the evidence that orders were placed there. A high that held once (or, stronger, multiple times — "respects it, respects it, respects it... it taps into it a bunch", Video 2, [67:20]) shows sellers entering against it and breakout stops stacking above it.

### 5b. A level has NO liquidity when it *itself swept* liquidity and price has not yet returned to respect it

> "If we have swept out this high and moved to the downside, why would there be any liquidity above this high? There wouldn't be. All we've now done is literally just ran this high to the upside. Price has not approached the high and moved away yet, meaning there shouldn't be any liquidity above this high. Obviously, nothing's 100%." (Video 1, [28:44]–[29:25])

A swing point that was *created by* a liquidity sweep (a stop hunt wick) and then abandoned holds no resting orders — nobody entered there; the move through it consumed the orders that were there. These are safe to trade *against*:

- Video 2/3 give this a name: a **"liquidity block" (LB)** — "a low that has spiked out previous lows... I don't view this low that needs to go. It doesn't need to get ran" (Video 2, [51:49]; also [35:05], [56:12], [62:50]; Video 3, [2:27], [5:24], [8:05], [12:15]). The high-side version: "a high that has taken out a previous high" (Video 2, [67:41]).
- Because no liquidity rests beyond it, "there's no liquidity below this low. Therefore, why would we run this low? So we use that to your advantage. We tap into these areas, take entries off them" (Video 3, [12:15]). It is where the stop loss hides (see file 03).
- Caveat: the exemption is temporary. Once price later *returns to and respects* an LB and moves away, it acquires liquidity like any other level: "we've respected this blue box, it moved away. So yeah, now there's liquidity there... But until it moves away, that is then a safe place" (Video 1, [76:22]–[76:54]).

### 5c. Practical consequence

Marking a chart is therefore a filter, not a highlighter. The "novice trader... is going to think there's highs and highs and lows all over my chart. What do I do? Am I going to sell above this, buy below this?" (Video 2, [17:55]–[18:13]). The answer: mark **only** levels the market has confirmed by respect-and-move-away; explicitly de-mark (treat as LBs) the swing points that were themselves sweeps. "You want to make sure there is liquidity above this high before selling, because typically what happens is you'll just get a pullback and a continuation. Boom. Trade invalidated" (Video 2, [25:49]–[26:14]).

## 6. Reactions inside the trap zone are false

Once liquidity has been identified below (bullish case), any bounce *from above* that liquidity — from retail POIs, order blocks, imbalances, 50% levels — is treated as **false and short-lived**, existing only to induce more participants: "if there is reactions in this blue box, in this area here, I understand it to be false and it's going to be short-lived; it's just to induce buyers to build liquidity at the lows. I will never look to take longs above the lows, only below" (Video 1, [9:33]–[10:12]). Video 3's phrasing: "any bearish moves, deem them to be false, use them to find a buy opportunity back up" (Video 3, [3:43]; also Video 2, [37:58], [50:03]).

## 7. Internal vs external liquidity

- **External liquidity** = the top and bottom of the current range (the higher-timeframe pools). **Internal liquidity** = swing points formed *inside* the range on the way to external (Video 1, [27:37]–[28:09]).
- Usage rule: **"I'm taking entries off internal liquidity taken, targeting external"** (Video 1, [28:09]–[28:44]). A false sweep of an internal level is the entry trigger; the external pool is the destination.
- "Typically once external is taken, there's reversals that occur" — external sweeps mark range turning points (Video 1, [28:09]–[28:44]).

## 8. Fractality

The whole model is scale-invariant: "it's fractal, right? So this could happen on the daily, it could happen all the way down to the one minute" (Video 1, [12:06]–[12:37]; Video 2, [2:34], [8:54]–[9:11], [41:10]: "it's all fractal all the way down to the 5-second if you want"). Standard deployment: higher timeframe identifies the draw (direction/bias), lower timeframe supplies the entry (Video 1, [11:32]–[12:06]; Video 2, [23:01], [39:32]; Video 3, [15:11]–[15:54]).

## 9. Anti-pattern warning (from the author himself)

Marco explicitly warns against treating his whiteboard diagrams as a chart pattern to template-match: "I don't want people to view it as like a pattern... don't just print out this diagram, go to your chart and just try to find this exact scenario, cuz it won't work like that. You need to understand what I'm trying to draw out here" (Video 1, [12:06]). Same warning in Video 2 ([29:29]: "I'm not a pattern-based trader... You need to make sure you always have a reason, a logic") and Video 3 ([7:35]: "Just because we have taken out this low does not mean we need to buy right away. That is pattern trading. We do not do that."). Every trade must be anchored in *where orders actually rest*, not in shape recognition.
